It started with a simple dream.

I wanted to start a venture studio: a company whose job is to start other companies.

So I got into VC Lab, a program that trains new venture investors. I studied how the money decides.

And I learned something.
Most investors are guessing.

Good guesses, smart guesses. But still guesses. I said to myself: no. There has to be a better way.

The better way was sitting on a shelf.

Every year the U.S. government spends billions of dollars on research. Rockets, materials, chips, sensors, medicine.

There are more than 300 federal labs doing this work.

When the research is done, most of it just sits there.
Paid for. Proven. Waiting.

That's called federal IP: inventions the government owns and lets companies license.

300 labs.
I picked one.

I narrowed it down to NASA, for three reasons:

Practical. It was made to work in the real world, and in space.

Dual use. It works for everyday business and for government missions.

Already tested. Inventions are rated on a ladder called TRL, from 1 (an idea on paper) to 9 (proven on real missions). The NASA patents I worked with sit at level 4 or higher: tested in a lab, not just a sketch.

Technology Readiness Level (TRL), the 1 to 9 scale NASA created.

One patent is a brick.

Here's what studying NASA's patents taught me. One patent on its own is weak. A competitor just walks around it.

In business, the thing that keeps competitors out is called a moat. One brick is not a moat.

But stack the bricks the right way, and you get a wall nobody can walk around.

That's what I did with Titanym: NASA patents stacked into a design for the first U.S. foundry that makes two kinds of advanced chips on one sapphire base.

Then the real question hit me.
Why stop at one?

NASA reuses its inventions across many fields. The same idea can live in a chip, a robot, and a satellite.

So instead of one company, I designed a family of them, from the smallest part to the biggest machine.

That's called vertically integrated. The family can make its own parts, from the chip up.

I'm a systems architect.

That means I design how big things fit together. I spent more than a year vetting this IP, patent by patent, checking what stacks with what.

I never planned to run every company myself. A venture studio brings in experts to lead each one.

But finding those people takes time. So I didn't wait.

I designed the solutions myself.

How do you prove hardware with no product?

Hardware takes money to make, so it's hard to prove. I needed someone serious to look at the designs.

So I submitted them to Tradewinds, the Defense Department's marketplace for vetted solutions, run by its AI office (the CDAO).

0 assessed awardable

Awardable means government reviewers assessed it as ready for agencies to buy.

And 2 of those 12, Titanym and Ravionics, were also assessed awardable by DARPA, the Pentagon's research agency.

And still, I had nothing.

No product. No team. A stack of patents and an idea I had carried for years.

Then AI changed the math.

For the first time, one architect could turn designs into working software.
So I turned the idea I had always pictured into software.

That idea is Radius.

Radius is the one shared layer every USG company runs on. Picture a building with five floors:

Products on top: each company's own software.

People and AI agents who ask to do things.

USG Radius in the middle: the guard and the signed logbook. Ask, check, act, record.

A shared toolbox: design, simulation, AI memory. Set up once, used by everyone.

Machines at the bottom: chips, robots, satellites, sensors. That's where Crucible, the hardware side, comes in.

Nothing moves without asking first.

Inside Radius is the Action Gate. Before any company's software does anything, it asks: am I allowed?

Radius checks who is asking, what they want, and whether they have permission. Then it answers yes or no.

Every answer goes into a signed logbook. Each page is chained to the one before it, so nobody can quietly change history.

Advisory or approved, allowed or denied. It's all on the record.

Who guards the guard?

Radius checks every action. But Radius also runs dozens of outside tools. Any one of them could carry something bad.

So we designed our own cybersecurity company: Vigilantyx.

Every new tool and every update lands in quarantine first. Vigilantyx checks it before anything is installed. If it fails, it never gets in.

Nothing gets in without a check. Not even our own updates.

Vigilantyx isn't one of the 12 awardable. We needed it, so we made it. It's a big part of what makes Radius trustworthy.

This isn't a drawing. It's running.

Radius runs today on its own private server. The family has 19 companies. 8 are running on Radius: their software asks it before it acts. The other 11 are designed.

Daedyl, the chip design company, was first. Every one of its design jobs asks Radius first.

The machines are already moving, in simulation.

Before you spend money on metal, you test it in a computer. That's a simulation.

Simulations are already running for many of these designs, especially McCoy, Easley, Galleion and Arddeon.

Everything here is simulation only. Nothing on Radius can command a real aircraft, boat or robot.

The four panels are drawings that stand for each simulation. They are not recordings.

Here's exactly where we stand.

Eight steps take a deep-tech company from a shelf to the real world. I cleared the first four with no money and no team.

The next step is people: experts to lead each company.
Then real hardware, with Crucible and capital.

Crucible: the same idea, for hardware.

Radius is one shared foundation for software. Every company runs on it, so no company builds its own.

Crucible does the same for hardware. One shared place where designs become real, tested parts, for every company in the family.

Every part is assembled, stress tested and given its own record before it leaves. The same rule as Radius: nothing moves without a check.

And it starts with chips.

Not one factory. A network.

Crucible isn't one giant plant. It's a network of small production cells. The first one is planned for Rancho Cucamonga, California.

Our customers aren't in one place. The DoD, DARPA, the Navy and commercial buyers are spread across the whole country.

So Crucible is designed to put a cell close to the customer, wherever it's needed.

Every cell runs the same playbook through Radius. Same designs, same tests, same records.
